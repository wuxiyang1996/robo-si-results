Task infrastructure · Evaluation protocol

Results must be comparable,
reproducible and leak-free.

This page fixes how evaluations are run and how results are reported: budgets, scoring delivery snapshots at budget points, normalising and aggregating scores, statistics, baselines, versions and reproducibility, leakage prevention and what is published. The verifier itself is on the scoring and verification page; the feedback an agent gets during a session is in section 05 of the isolated sandbox.

01 · Budgets

Simulated steps and tokens are the primary budgets; wall-clock time is only a cap

"More than 4 hours, no upper limit" describes task difficulty; evaluation also needs a budget that can be compared. Wall-clock time depends heavily on hardware and load: with the GPU busy with other work, H1 takes 0.67 s per control step, so the same agent on another machine or at another time can run several times fewer experiments. Therefore:

BudgetHow it is countedRole
Simulated stepsCounted on the environment side: control steps advanced by command, before and after resetsPrimary: how many physical experiments the agent can run
TokensLLM input and output tokens metered independently by the trusted proxy, plus their costPrimary: how much the agent can think
ObservationsCounted on the environment sidePrevents unlimited sensor reads
ResetsA cap in development modePrevents trial and error from replacing understanding
Wall clockTimed by the launcherA cap only, against hangs; never used for comparison
  • The development session ends when either primary budget is used up.
  • Every budget is metered by the environment side or the trusted proxy; nothing the agent reports counts.
  • Each task card states the amounts and roughly what wall-clock time they correspond to on the reference hardware.

02 · Delivery snapshots scored at budget points

Report how the score grows with budget, not just one final score

  • During the development session, the trusted side snapshots the workspace at several budget points and records the budget used so far. Budget points are defined in simulated steps and tokens, for example the amounts that correspond to about 4, 12 and 24 hours on the reference hardware.
  • Every snapshot is scored automatically in grading mode on sealed new episodes (3 grading runs per episode, section 04), so one evaluation yields a score-versus-budget curve, and the 3 evaluations yield three.
  • The agent does not know when snapshots are taken; it simply keeps its current best program at the agreed entry point (see section 06 of the isolated sandbox).
  • EmbodiedSWE also stamps each submission snapshot with wall-clock time and transcript position; here the budgets metered on the environment side are used instead.

03 · How results are reported

Per task and per layer first, then aggregated

ItemHow it is computed
Raw scoreEach graded episode's process score (peak progress, averaged over its 3 grading runs), averaged over episodes
Success rateThe mean of each graded episode's success (0 / 1, averaged over its 3 grading runs)
Normalised scoreRaw score divided by the reference expert's score (a no-op is 0). Tasks whose reference does not cover every stage report raw scores only
LayersLayers 1, 2 and 3 reported separately, plus their equally weighted mean
Across tasksEvery task weighted equally: the mean normalised score (raw score where there is none) and the mean success rate; never weighted by episode count
StagesThe mean best value per stage, showing where agents get stuck
RepeatsEach of the 3 evaluations' scores, their mean and range; the largest spread across the 3 grading runs; the number of non-reproducible episodes
CostSimulated steps, tokens and their cost, observations, resets, wall clock
Flags and voidsNumber of flagged episodes and why; number of void episodes

Every number comes from automatic scoring; every report states the task and verifier versions (section 06).

Separate screening from execution scores

Report counts for witnessed_full, screen_pass, unknown and rejected, total draws, budget exhaustion and rejection reasons. Skill probes and full executions have separate denominators. Screening passes earn no task success or full reference score. Self-generated candidates may be filtered by predeclared rules; retain every prescribed benchmark sample, without silently removing unknowns/failures or replacing seeds. Any subset report must include the full sampling ledger, predeclared selection rules and state counts. Existing agent-evaluation and grading repeats remain; they do not require solving each random instance with a complete reference first.

04 · Statistics

Episode counts, three repeats and confidence intervals

  • Graded episodes: we suggest at least 10 sealed episodes per layer, stratified by family. With too few episodes scores swing widely (the H1 sandbox documentation notes this too).
  • Evaluation repeated 3 times (required): every agent runs 3 independent evaluations per task, each starting from a new development session. An agent's own randomness is often larger than the episodes', so a single evaluation does not represent its ability. All three scores are reported, with their mean and range.
  • Grading re-run 3 times (required): every delivery snapshot is scored automatically 3 times on the same sealed episodes. GPU physics is not bitwise repeatable, and the re-runs test whether the evaluation output reproduces: success must agree across the three, and per-episode scores must stay within the tolerance (for example ≤0.05, fixed in the task card). The episode score is the mean of the three.
  • When it does not reproduce: an episode whose three scores differ beyond the tolerance is flagged as not reproducible and still scored as their mean; the task or verifier is not robust to physics noise, and the maintainers fix it, with automatic re-grading in the next version.
  • Confidence intervals: bootstrap over episodes and sessions for 95% intervals; agents whose intervals overlap are not ranked against each other.
  • No best-of: grading runs are averaged and every evaluation counts; nothing is re-run to pick the best.

Episode counts and tolerances are suggestions that each task fixes in its task card according to its variance; the three repeats are required. All are frozen before evaluation.

05 · Baselines

Besides the no-op and the reference, standard agent baselines

BaselinePurpose
No-opLower anchor; must score 0
Reference expertUpper anchor; proves solvability and validates the verifier
Standard agent configurationsPinned coding agents (for example the version-pinned Claude Code the agent image can install) with a default prompt, run under the same budgets. They give a reference for difficulty and discrimination, and test the assumption that a strong agent needs more than 4 hours
Simple scripted baseline (optional)A fixed script that uses only the task-provided skills and no perception. It tests whether a fixed routine can already score

Baseline results are published with each task version and re-run after revisions. The difficulty pilot of the contribution process uses the standard agent configurations.

06 · Versions and reproducibility

Every result traces back to fixed versions

Version numbers and hashes

  • Task version major.minor: changes that can alter scores (scene, predicates, thresholds, weights, monitor rules, simulation settings, actuation and sensing delays) bump the major version; documentation-only changes bump the minor version.
  • Every scored result records the task version, the verifier code hash, the simulator version (such as Genesis 1.4.3), asset hashes, the agent image's content hash, the hardware, and (on the trusted side only) the seeds.

Physics non-determinism on the GPU

GPU physics is not bitwise repeatable. Re-runs during evaluation are in section 04; re-runs at task acceptance in section 03 of task acceptance.

Automatic re-grading after revisions

  • The trusted side keeps every delivery snapshot. After a major version bump, all snapshots are re-graded automatically on the new version; old results are kept and labelled with their version.
  • Every task keeps a change log: what changed, why (for example which flagged episode revealed which problem) and how results were affected.

07 · Leakage and publication

Private material never leaves the trusted side; only aggregates are published

Public and private

MaterialPublic development tasksPrivate evaluation tasks
Instruction, robot and sensor specifications, SDKPublicPublic (the agent gets them anyway)
Task descriptions, design documentsPublicOnly the goal and category; never what must be discovered
Reference expert solution, official trajectoriesMay be publicPrivate
Verifier code, predicate thresholds, weights, negative cases, monitor thresholdsMay be publicPrivate
Sealed seedsPrivatePrivate, changed every evaluation round
Task check records, flagged episodesMaintainers onlyMaintainers only

Keeping it out of training data

  • Private material lives only in private repositories or on the trusted side, never in a public repository, web page or paper appendix. robo-si is currently a private repository.
  • Every task file carries a canary string to detect training-data contamination; Terminal-Bench's task files carry such a string, the local Harbor task does not yet.
  • The agent's network reaches only the LLM API (section 03 of the isolated sandbox), so it cannot search for task material during evaluation.

What is published after an evaluation

  • Published: per task and layer, raw and normalised scores, success rates, confidence intervals and cost; mean best value per stage; numbers of flagged and void episodes; task and verifier versions.
  • Not published (private tasks): per-episode videos and logs, predicate details, sealed seeds, delivered programs and transcripts. Publishing them would let the next round overfit the verifier.
  • Public development tasks may publish per-episode videos and logs.
  • Which feedback the agent gets during a session and which stays on the trusted side is in section 05 of the isolated sandbox.
